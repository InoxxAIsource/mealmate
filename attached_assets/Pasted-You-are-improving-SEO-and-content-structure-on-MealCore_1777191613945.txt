Instruction 11: Human Tone Writing Standard
Applies to: All blog posts (new and existing) + all condition landing pages

## WRITING TONE & VOICE — MANDATORY FOR ALL CONTENT

All blog posts and landing page copy must be rewritten or written to sound like a 
real Indian nutritionist or health writer — not an AI assistant. 

The target reader is an Indian woman (25–45) who is tired of googling the same 
thing every week, has tried diets that didn't work, and wants honest, practical 
advice from someone who actually understands her food, her kitchen, and her life.

Write for her. Not for search engines. Not for a medical textbook.

---

### THE VOICE TO WRITE IN

Think: a knowledgeable friend who is also a dietitian. She speaks plainly, 
acknowledges real struggles, uses the food names her reader actually uses at home, 
and doesn't wrap every sentence in qualifications.

She says "ragi roti" not "Eleusine coracana flatbread."
She says "your cravings will get worse" not "adherence to dietary interventions 
may be compromised."
She says "here's what I'd actually tell a patient" not "it is recommended that 
individuals consider..."

---

### FORBIDDEN — AI WRITING PATTERNS TO REMOVE

When rewriting existing content or writing new content, remove and replace 
every instance of the following:

FORBIDDEN PHRASES (never use these):
- "It is important to note that..."
- "In conclusion..."
- "Furthermore," / "Moreover," / "Additionally,"
- "It is worth mentioning..."
- "When it comes to [topic],"
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "This comprehensive guide will..."
- "Let's delve into..."
- "Without further ado..."
- "Navigating [health condition] can be challenging..."
- "Harnessing the power of..."
- "Unlock your potential..."
- Any sentence that starts with "Certainly" or "Absolutely"
- "Plays a crucial role in..."
- "It goes without saying..."
- "As we explore..."

FORBIDDEN STRUCTURES:
- Do NOT open every section with a definition. Real writers assume the reader 
  knows what PCOS is. They get to the point.
- Do NOT list every paragraph as "First... Second... Third... Finally..."
- Do NOT use 4-adjective sentences: "a powerful, anti-inflammatory, hormone-
  balancing, nutrient-dense food."
- Do NOT hedge everything: "may potentially help in some cases." Say what you 
  know, cite what you're uncertain about.
- Do NOT make every paragraph exactly 3 sentences. Vary the rhythm.
- Do NOT end sections with "By following these tips, you can improve your 
  health journey." That's filler. Cut it.

---

### WHAT HUMAN WRITING LOOKS LIKE — RULES

1. START WITH THE PROBLEM, NOT THE DEFINITION
   AI: "PCOS, or Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, is a hormonal disorder that affects..."
   Human: "If you've been Googling 'why am I gaining weight despite eating right,' 
           PCOS might be the answer no one's given you yet."

2. USE SHORT SENTENCES AFTER LONG ONES
   Vary rhythm. One long sentence that explains something complex. Then a short 
   one. Like this. It keeps readers moving.

3. ACKNOWLEDGE THE READER'S REAL LIFE
   AI: "Dietary adherence is critical for PCOS management."
   Human: "Here's the hard truth: even the best meal plan doesn't work if you 
           can't stick to it past Day 3. So we'll keep this realistic."

4. USE INDIAN CONTEXT NATURALLY, NOT AS A FEATURE
   AI: "Indian cuisine offers numerous PCOS-friendly food options."
   Human: "Your dadi's methi sabzi? That's actually one of the best things you 
           can eat for PCOS. Some traditional Indian cooking got it right without 
           anyone calling it a 'superfood.'"

5. BE SPECIFIC, NOT COMPREHENSIVE
   AI: "There are many foods that can help with PCOS symptoms including millets, 
           legumes, leafy greens, spices, omega-3 rich foods, and more."
   Human: "If you eat one thing differently this week, make it this: swap your 
           white roti for bajra roti. That single change can stabilise your blood 
           sugar for the entire morning."

6. CITE STUDIES LIKE A PERSON, NOT A ROBOT
   AI: "Studies show that cinnamon supplementation can improve insulin sensitivity 
           by up to 19% (Journal of Nutritional Science, 2019)."
   Human: "A study in the Journal of Nutritional Science found that half a 
           teaspoon of cinnamon a day improved insulin sensitivity by 19%. Half 
           a teaspoon — that's it. Put it in your chai."

7. USE CONTRACTIONS
   Write "don't" not "do not." Write "you'll" not "you will." Write "it's" 
   not "it is." Exception: medical/clinical terms where formality matters.

8. EARN THE CTA, DON'T JUST APPEND IT
   AI: "MealCoreAI can help you create a personalised meal plan. Sign up today."
   Human: "Figuring all of this out yourself, every single day, is exhausting. 
           That's why we built MealCoreAI — so you don't have to. Your plan is 
           ready in 90 seconds."

### TONE CALIBRATION SCALE

Use this as a check before publishing any content:

Score 1 (AI) → Score 5 (Human)

1 — Reads like a Wikipedia article. Formal definitions, passive voice, 
    no personality.
2 — Reads like a hospital pamphlet. Accurate but cold. No reader 
    acknowledgment.
3 — Reads like a health blog. Friendly but generic. Could be about anyone 
    in any country.
4 — Reads like a good Indian health blog. Uses real food names, 
    acknowledges the reader's life, specific advice.
5 — Reads like a message from your nutritionist friend. Specific, warm, 
    direct, Indian, practical, honest.

Target: Every piece of content must be a 4 or 5. 
If you read it back and it sounds like it came from ChatGPT, rewrite it.

---

### LENGTH AND FORMATTING IN HUMAN WRITING

- Blog posts: 1,200–2,000 words. Not shorter (thin content), not longer 
  (padding is an AI habit too).
- Paragraphs: 2–4 sentences max. Single-sentence paragraphs are fine for 
  emphasis. Walls of text are not.
- Bullet lists: Use them only for genuinely list-like content (foods to eat, 
  day-by-day plans). Do NOT bullet-point your entire article. Prose is more 
  readable and more human.
- Headers: H2 every 300–400 words. Make them specific and interesting, not 
  generic. "Foods to Eat" is weak. "The 5 Indian Foods Worth Eating Every Day 
  If You Have PCOS" is strong.
- Bold text: Use sparingly — for one key phrase per section, not every 
  other sentence.

### REGIONAL VOICE NOTE

Writers should specifically reference Indian cultural contexts naturally in 
the flow of the article — not as a tokenistic add-on at the end. This means:

- Mentioning festival food challenges ("what to eat during Navratri fasting 
  if you have PCOS")
- Referencing family pressure ("cooking one meal for the whole family, not 
  just yourself")
- Using Hindi/regional food terms naturally without always italicising or 
  over-explaining them
- Acknowledging the dietitian shortage outside metro cities
- Referencing the reality that most Indian women self-manage their conditions

These details make the content feel written by someone who lives in India, 
not someone who researched India.
